01:00

Structured logging

Terminal — log tail 2 min

One JSON object per line — no free text, nothing needing a regex. The fields worth pointing at: correlation_id, the same on every line of one request. duration_ms and status_code on the completion line. And trace_id and span_id, which I'll come back to.

One deliberate deviation from the plan, and I'd rather say it as a decision than have it look like an oversight. The plan said request_id; I used correlation_id. The job I'll show you at the end has no HTTP request behind it but still needs correlating through the same logger — so the field is named for what it does, and context_type (http, job, or system) tells me the source.

Underneath it's AsyncLocalStorage rather than a request-scoped provider, chosen on Day 2 for that Day 8 reason: a request-scoped provider needs a request to scope to, and the cron hasn't got one. When I built the job, nothing downstream had to change.

And when there's no context the field is omitted, not placeholdered — same as trace_id, because W3C treats an all-zeroes trace id as invalid. A line never carries an id you can't look up.

Grafana → Terminal 3 min

Look at Request rate by route specifically. Each line is one route. Not one URL — one route. And that distinction is the decision I'd defend hardest on this whole dashboard, so rather than assert it, let me show you what's actually stored underneath.

Drop to the terminal for the proof

Hazard 5 · do not say "135" while this output is on screen This command shows only the http_requests_total series — that's 2 right now (/ and unmatched), or 4–5 if you've exercised the debug endpoints. The 135 in your Day 3 journal is a different number: the whole registry projected for the finished service — "~15 counter series and ~120 histogram series." Quote them as two separate figures or the screen contradicts you. (This is exactly the Day 5 failure: the script said two lines, the panel had four.)

Two series. And the second one is route="unmatched" with over a thousand requests behind it — bot probes, every one a different URL, all collapsed into one series.

That's the decision. Every distinct label value is a separate time series Prometheus stores, indexes and updates. Label with the raw URL and series count becomes a function of traffic — a number the internet picks for me. Label with the route template and it's bounded by routes times status codes I can actually emit, which I get by reading my own code.

Scaled to the finished service that's about fifteen counter series and a hundred and twenty histogram series — around a hundred and thirty-five, which is nothing. Labelled by raw URL: ten locations times three hundred and sixty-five dates, so roughly a hundred and thirty-one thousand. A thousandfold, and it grows every day rather than settling.

One thing I'd add: the in-flight gauge is the only one of the three that means anything during a hang. The counter and histogram are driven by completion — if the database seizes, nothing completes, both halves of an error ratio go to zero, and zero over zero crosses no threshold. The gauge is driven by arrival, which always happens.

06:00

Where the numbers come from

Prometheus UI 2 min
Screen — /targets, then /rules

Two targets, both up. Prometheus pulls — the app exposes current values and never pushes anywhere; it doesn't know Prometheus exists. Those values live in the process heap, so they reset on restart, which is why you always rate() a counter rather than subtracting raw values. rate() treats a decrease as a reset and compensates.

Six rules loaded. Two things about how they're written. The error rule is a ratio, not an absolute count — an absolute threshold encodes whatever traffic you had when you wrote it, and then it's wrong in the direction that trains people to ignore the page.

And RequestsStuckInFlight is separate for the reason I just gave: during a hang the ratio is blind and the gauge is the only thing still moving.

If you asked how fast I'd know: six to ten minutes depending on severity. Not instantly — there's a scrape interval and a for window, and both are deliberate.

08:00

Distributed tracing

Terminal → Jaeger 3 min
  1. Terminal — fire both endpoints
  2. Wait ~5s — narrate it
  3. Jaeger UI — the waterfall
  4. Terminal — grep the logs by that id

You end back in the terminal on purpose — pasting the trace id from Jaeger into your own logs is the strongest thirty seconds in this section.

1 · Terminal — two endpoints, one variable
curl -s -o /dev/null -w 'status=%{http_code} in %{time_total}s\n' localhost:3000/downstream-immediate curl -s -o /dev/null -w 'status=%{http_code} in %{time_total}s\n' localhost:3000/fire-and-forget

Two endpoints differing in exactly one variable — whether the downstream call is awaited. The first returns 200 in about fifty milliseconds. The second returns 202 in about two, because it doesn't wait.

2 · Wait — and say why you're waiting
Hazard 3 · give it a genuine five seconds Measured: at 3 seconds the trace has not arrived and Jaeger will show you a different request. At 5 it's there. Don't hide the pause — it is the point you're about to make.

It won't be there instantly, and the reason is worth saying: a span reaches Jaeger when two separate things are both true. It's been ended, and its batch has been flushed. Two gates, and they fail independently.

3 · Jaeger UI — show the tree
# pre-open this tab; just refresh and click the newest http://localhost:16686/search?service=booking-api&operation=GET%20%2Fdownstream-immediate

There it is — ten spans, and two services in one trace: booking-api at the top, notifier nested underneath it. The instrumentation injects a W3C traceparent header on the way out and reads it on the way in, so the second service joins without either side agreeing a custom header name.

And that's what a trace gives me that correlation_id didn't: structure. A flat id gives a set of lines. span_id plus parent_span_id plus duration gives a tree — what called what, and how long relative to its parent. You can't recover parentage from timestamps.

4 · Back to terminal — join the two tools
Copy the trace id out of Jaeger, paste it in place of <id>. Six lines come back, from both services.
docker compose logs --no-log-prefix | grep <id>

Same id, in the logs, from both services — so I can go from a slow trace in Jaeger straight to every line that request produced, without knowing in advance what I was looking for.

Which raises the obvious question: why keep both ids? Sampling. This runs at a hundred percent because the volume is tiny, but at real volume you sample — and logs never are. So a log line can carry a trace_id whose trace was never exported, and pasting it into Jaeger returns nothing. The correlation id still works.

And on the fire-and-forget one: if that call fails after the 202 has gone out, an unhandled rejection takes the process down and BatchSpanProcessor loses its whole buffer — not just the failed span, every healthy request in that batch too. That's the second gate failing, and it's why the rejection is caught.

The silent failure

Terminal 4 min · the centre of the day

Not a tail — four one-shot commands, all in the same terminal, run in this order. The order is the argument: it says "healthy", then "still healthy", then shows it isn't, then shows what caught it. Don't reorder it. stepper beats 13 → 14 → 15 → 16

  1. Its own words — the log lines
  2. The absence — zero errors
  3. The truth — gauge climbing
  4. The catch — one alert fires

This is the scenario from my interview — a scheduled job that fails without erroring.

1 · What the job says about itself
docker compose logs booking-api --no-log-prefix | grep '"message":"job completed"' \ | tail -3 | jq -c '{level, outcome, swept: .result.swept, tracked: .result.tracked}'

Every run: level info, outcome success. And the field that matters is swept — it is zero. The job ran, did no work at all, and reported success. By its own account it is perfectly healthy.

Hazard 6 · swept is the discriminator — tracked is not tracked climbs at three per tick in the healthy state too, because the seeder runs either way. Saying "and the number it tracks keeps climbing" describes both cases equally, so it is evidence of nothing. A constant cannot explain a variable. Point at swept: 0 and nothing else. (Healthy reads swept: 3, tracked: 66; silent reads swept: 0, tracked: 66.)
2 · The absence — and this is the whole point
curl -s localhost:3000/metrics | grep '^job_runs_total' docker compose logs booking-api --no-log-prefix | grep -cE 'level.:.error'

The success counter climbing, no failures recorded. And zero error-level lines in the entire log — not a small number, zero. There is nothing to alert on. If you were grepping for errors you would find nothing and conclude the system was fine.

3 · Meanwhile, the world is wrong
curl -s localhost:3000/metrics | grep '^bookings_expired_pending'

That's expired bookings that should have been released and weren't — desks locked that shouldn't be. The job reports success while this climbs.

4 · What catches it
curl -s localhost:9090/api/v1/rules \ | jq -r '.data.groups[].rules[] | " \(.name) \(.state)"'

The alert keyed on state is firing. The one keyed on whether the job ran is silent — correctly, because it did run and it did report success. The principle: key on the absence of an expected success, not the presence of an error. If there were an error to log, it wouldn't be a silent failure.

Three decisions inside I'd defend. The state gauge is a collect() callback, not something the job sets — a signal written by the component it watches freezes at its last healthy value when that component dies. collect() is evaluated by the scrape, so the reporter is alive by definition.

The success counter is in the try, not a finally — and this one nearly caught me. A span goes in finally because it must close on every path. A success counter is the opposite: reachable on exactly one path. In a finally it counts failures as successes and silently disables the day's alerting.

And the staleness rule is time() minus last-success rather than rate(...) == 0, because rate() needs two samples — a job that stops after a deploy gives an empty result, which == 0 never matches. With an absent() arm, because a failed scrape returns no data rather than a frozen value, and without it a crash-looping container is perfectly silent.

If he pushes on the threshold — concede it cleanly: five is a claim about my traffic. A count threshold encodes your traffic; an age threshold encodes your SLO, and is the same rule at 1 location and at 200.
15:00

The bug the demo found in itself

Terminal 2 min
Volunteer this. Nobody would have known. stepper beats 17–18

One more, and I'd rather raise it than leave it sitting there — it's a bug I shipped into my own demo.

First time I ran this, two alerts fired, the gauge climbed, the story looked perfect. One of those alerts was a false positive, and the sweep it was complaining about was running flawlessly.

job is Prometheus's own label — the server stamps it on every series it scrapes, from the scrape config. When a target exposes a label the server already owns, honor_labels decides who wins, and it defaults to false. So my label lost and was silently renamed exported_job.

Nothing errored. /metrics looked correct. But my alert selected on job="expiry-sweep", which now matched nothing — so absent() returned 1 and the rule fired permanently. What caught it was running the selector directly and getting an empty result.

That's the line I'd take from the whole two weeks: a firing alert is not evidence that the alert works. Fixed by renaming to job_name. The same collision had existed on service since Day 3, harmless only because both values happened to be identical.

Close — name your own gaps

You, to camera 1 min

Let me close by naming what isn't here, before you find it.

No Alertmanager — rules reach firing and notify nobody. And what I'd add first isn't routing, it's a deadman's switch, because this stack can't detect its own death: if Prometheus dies the series don't go to zero, they stop existing, and that's indistinguishable from a quiet night.

No PII or secret redaction in the logger. Tracing at a hundred percent with no sampling — right at this volume, wrong at any real one. No /health separate from /metrics, so the healthcheck renders the whole registry every ten seconds.

And the store is in-memory rather than Postgres — a deliberate scope call, not something I ran out of time for. The instrumentation is identical either way; with a real table the gauge's collect() callback picks up a query cost inside the scrape, which is the part worth knowing about.

If he asksThe spine of your answer
Why Winston, not pino? pino pre-compiles its serialiser and hands the string to sonic-boom for non-blocking writes, with transports on a worker thread; Winston's format chain is synchronous. What you'd give up going the other way: format.errors({ stack: true }), a composable independently-testable correlationFormat, and same-process pretty-printing. Then the turn: that async buffer dies on a hard exit — which is exactly the failure class this plan is about, so the synchronous transport is a feature here. Close with the volume at which you'd switch.
Why a metric when the log already has status_code and duration_ms? A histogram is ~2 GB/year and flat in traffic; the equivalent logs are ~1.9 TB/year and linear. A bucket is a pre-aggregated counter, a log line is the whole event. And a dropped log line is indistinguishable from a request that never happened — where a missed scrape leaves a visible gap. The honest other half: write-time aggregation forecloses the questions you didn't predict, which is why you run both.

  • When you don't know: "that's a guess — here's how I'd check it." On Day 6 "I don't know" scored better than a plausible story.
  • When corrected, say it back out loud. It sticks, and it shows him it landed.
